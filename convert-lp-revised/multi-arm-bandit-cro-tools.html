01 · Bandit strategies

Both Bayesian and Frequentist bandits, built into one platform

This is what actually sets Convert's multi-arm bandits apart. Convert runs them on three sampling methods — Thompson Sampling, which is Bayesian, plus Epsilon-Greedy and UCB, which are Frequentist. The sampling method is the statistical approach, so you get both without bolting a strategy onto a separate engine.

  • Thompson Sampling — the Bayesian method, allocating traffic by each variation's probability of being best
  • Epsilon-Greedy and UCB — the Frequentist methods, balancing exploration against the current leader
  • Both approaches ship on Convert Pro and Enterprise, no upsell and no separate engine to configure

03 · A/B and bandits together

You don't have to pick one — Convert runs bandits and A/B tests side by side

Reach for a multi-arm bandit to protect revenue during a time-sensitive window; reach for a fixed-horizon A/B test when you need a clean, defensible result for the decision log. Common questions about multi-arm bandit testing

A standard A/B test splits traffic evenly between variants for the full duration of the test, then declares a winner at the end. A multi-arm bandit test dynamically reallocates traffic toward better-performing variations as data accumulates, so stronger-performing variations receive more traffic while the test is still running.

The practical difference: with a standard A/B test, you pay a fixed "cost of exploration" for the entire test duration. With a multi-arm bandit, that cost decreases over time as the algorithm exploits what it's learning. For high-traffic pages and time-sensitive campaigns, this can represent meaningful revenue recovered during the experiment itself.

Multi-arm bandits are most valuable when the cost of running a losing variant is high relative to the benefit of a statistically clean result. That typically means high-traffic pages, time-bounded campaigns (like seasonal promotions), and situations where you need to act quickly rather than wait for a traditional significance threshold.

Standard A/B tests are still preferable when you need a clean causal inference, when your traffic is lower and the algorithm has less signal to work with, or when your organization needs a methodologically conservative result for regulatory or compliance reasons. In many programs, both approaches run concurrently on different test types.

Convert supports both, and the choice lives in the sampling method itself. Thompson Sampling is the Bayesian approach; Epsilon-Greedy and UCB (Upper Confidence Bound) are Frequentist. Convert runs multi-arm bandits directly on these sampling methods, so you are not pairing a strategy with a separate statistical engine — you pick the method whose statistical approach fits your program, and that controls how traffic is redistributed.

Auto-Allocation optimizes the experience's selected primary goal using Conversion Rate, Revenue Per Visitor, or Average Products Per Visitor. Choose that primary goal carefully before launch, because it cannot be changed once the active MAB experience is enabled.

There is no fixed universal threshold. Auto-Allocation works best when the tested experience receives enough eligible traffic and conversions for the selected strategy to distinguish meaningful performance differences. The required volume depends on the number of variations, the baseline conversion rate, the conversion delay, and the expected effect size.

As a general guideline, if your current A/B tests take months to reach significance, a multi-arm bandit will also take time to converge. Convert's support team can help you assess whether a given test configuration makes sense for your traffic level before you start.

For bandit tests specifically, this matters because flicker can introduce a confounding variable: visitors may react to the disruption rather than the variant itself, which can corrupt the conversion signals the bandit algorithm uses to make reallocation decisions.

Sampled analytics in a bandit context creates a compounding problem: each reallocation cycle is made on incomplete data, and the errors accumulate over the duration of the test.

Sequential testing addresses the "peeking problem": if you monitor a standard A/B test continuously and make decisions based on early results, you inflate your false positive rate. Convert's Sequential test type lets you watch a running experiment and act on early signals without paying that penalty — which pairs naturally with an adaptive bandit that is reallocating traffic as evidence accumulates.

In Convert, you set the test type at the experiment level. Pairing Auto-Allocation with the Sequential test type before launch is what lets the bandit be monitored continuously as it favors stronger variations, rather than through an arbitrary peek that would distort your results.
