Convert supports three frameworks: Frequentist, Bayesian, and Sequential Frequentist. Frequentist is the standard null-hypothesis approach with configurable confidence thresholds. Bayesian gives you a probability-of-being-best metric that's easier to communicate to non-technical stakeholders. Choose between them based on your organization's decision framework, statistical preferences, and communication needs.

Sequential Testing is the most important for teams that check results regularly. It uses always-valid inference, so you can monitor results at any point during the test while controlling the risk of false positives associated with repeated peeking, which is the problem that arises when you peek at a fixed-sample Frequentist test before it reaches its target sample. All three are available on Convert Pro.

Flicker is what happens when an A/B testing script loads asynchronously. The browser renders the original page first, then the script fires and swaps in the variant. The visitor briefly sees the control before the variant appears. That brief flash is flicker, and it corrupts your experiment data in two ways: it pollutes the variant experience, and it can cause visitors to exit before the variant even renders, inflating your control's apparent performance.

Convert is designed to load synchronously via Akamai CDN. When the tracking code is installed synchronously per the recommended implementation, the script fires before the browser's first paint, so the variant is what the visitor sees from the start and visible flicker is minimized. This has been Convert's delivery approach since 2016, and it's why Convert's results tend to be more reliable than those from async-loading tools.

Sampled reporting means the platform counts a fraction of your visitors and projects that fraction up to an estimate. At high traffic volumes, sampling error is small. At lower traffic volumes, or when you're analyzing a niche segment like "returning visitors from paid search who viewed the pricing page," sampling errors compound and you end up making decisions on systematically incorrect data.
